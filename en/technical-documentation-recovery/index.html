AS-BUILT / LEGACY DOCUMENTATION

The drawings are missing, incomplete or no longer match the machine

The real problem is not missing paperwork. It is making maintenance, sourcing and modification decisions from information that no longer represents the asset.

When should technical documentation be reconstructed?

When original drawings were never supplied, files were lost, the equipment has been modified over years, or the available drawings cannot support a reliable repair, spare-parts enquiry or modification.

What is more useful than simply redrawing an old print?

An as-built package that reflects the actual machine: relevant geometry, interfaces, component relationships, manufacturing requirements and clearly identified uncertainty.

Why does this matter commercially?

Reliable technical data makes manufacturing quotations more comparable, reduces clarification loops and preserves knowledge that would otherwise remain with one supplier or one experienced employee.

Why do old drawings fail to match the actual machine?

Equipment may have been repaired, modified or fitted with replacement components over many years. Archive drawings can describe an earlier configuration, while the working machine has undocumented changes. Documentation recovery therefore starts with a comparison between available records and verified as-built evidence, not with indiscriminate redrawing of paper plans.

What belongs in a controlled documentation package?

Depending on scope, the result may include assembly structure, part drawings, editable CAD models, interfaces, bills of materials, identification of critical dimensions and a register of unresolved assumptions. Revision status and source references make later changes traceable. Manufacturing tolerances, material requirements and verification plans must be agreed where the available evidence is insufficient.

When a physical part is the only source, reverse engineering may be needed before drawings can be released for manufacture.
